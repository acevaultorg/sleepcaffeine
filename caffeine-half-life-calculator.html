How Caffeine Works in Your Body

When you consume caffeine, it is absorbed through the gastrointestinal tract and reaches peak blood concentration within 30 to 60 minutes. From there, it is metabolized primarily by the liver enzyme CYP1A2, following first-order elimination kinetics — meaning a constant fraction is removed per unit of time.

The standard half-life of caffeine in healthy adults is approximately 5 hours, though it can range from 1.5 to 9.5 hours depending on genetics, age, liver function, pregnancy status, and concurrent medications. This means that if you drink a coffee with 200 mg of caffeine at noon, you still have approximately 100 mg circulating at 5 PM and 50 mg at 10 PM.

Research consistently shows that caffeine levels above 50 mg can significantly disrupt sleep onset latency and reduce total sleep time, even if you do not feel "wired." This calculator uses the exponential decay formula C(t) = C₀ × 0.5^(t/t½) to model your caffeine levels over 24 hours.

Absorption Phase

Caffeine reaches peak plasma concentration 30-60 minutes after ingestion. Effects begin within 15 minutes. This calculator models from the time of consumption for simplicity.

Genetic Variation

Variants of the CYP1A2 gene determine whether you are a "fast" or "slow" metabolizer. About 50% of people carry the fast variant. Use the metabolism selector above to adjust.

Factors That Slow Metabolism

Pregnancy (half-life up to 15 hrs), oral contraceptives (doubles half-life), liver disease, grapefruit juice, and certain medications like fluvoxamine.

Factors That Speed Metabolism

Smoking (reduces half-life by ~50%), cruciferous vegetables, regular exercise, and being male (on average slightly faster clearance).

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does caffeine stay in your system?

▼
Caffeine can be detected in your bloodstream for up to 12 hours or more after consumption, but its effects diminish exponentially. With a typical 5-hour half-life, a 200 mg dose drops to 50 mg after 10 hours and roughly 12 mg after 20 hours. Most people will no longer "feel" caffeine once it drops below 20-30 mg, but even sub-perceptual levels above 50 mg can measurably reduce sleep quality in lab studies. Complete elimination (below 1 mg) typically takes 24-36 hours depending on the dose and your metabolism.

What is caffeine's half-life?

▼
Caffeine's half-life is the time it takes for the amount of caffeine in your bloodstream to be reduced by half. For the average healthy adult, this is approximately 5 hours, though it can range from 1.5 to 9.5 hours. This means if you consume 200 mg of caffeine, after 5 hours you will have about 100 mg remaining. After 10 hours, about 50 mg. After 15 hours, about 25 mg. The decay follows an exponential curve, not a linear one, which is why this calculator is useful for visualizing the true impact.

Why do some people metabolize caffeine faster?

▼
The primary reason is genetics. The CYP1A2 enzyme in the liver is responsible for metabolizing about 95% of ingested caffeine. People with the AA genotype of the CYP1A2 gene (roughly half the population) are "fast metabolizers" who break down caffeine significantly faster. Those with AC or CC genotypes are "slow metabolizers." Beyond genetics, lifestyle factors play a role: smokers metabolize caffeine nearly twice as fast, while pregnancy, oral contraceptives, and liver conditions can dramatically slow metabolism. Age, diet, and other medications also contribute to individual variation.

How much caffeine is too much?

▼
The FDA considers 400 mg of caffeine per day to be generally safe for most healthy adults — roughly four 8-ounce cups of coffee. However, "too much" depends on context. For sleep quality, having more than 50 mg of caffeine in your system at bedtime is considered disruptive. For cardiovascular effects, rapid consumption of 500+ mg can cause heart palpitations in sensitive individuals. Pregnant women are advised to limit intake to under 200 mg per day. Caffeine doses above 1,200 mg can be dangerous, and doses above 5,000-10,000 mg can be lethal, though reaching these levels from beverages alone is extremely difficult.

Does caffeine tolerance affect half-life?

▼
Tolerance and half-life are separate mechanisms. Tolerance refers to your brain's reduced sensitivity to caffeine's effects due to adenosine receptor upregulation — you need more caffeine to feel the same alertness. Half-life refers to how quickly your liver clears caffeine from your blood, which does not change significantly with regular use. However, regular caffeine consumers may have slightly induced CYP1A2 activity, marginally speeding metabolism. The practical consequence is that tolerant individuals may not feel wired but still have substantial caffeine levels disrupting their sleep architecture.

What affects caffeine metabolism speed?

▼
Several factors influence how quickly your body processes caffeine. Genetics (CYP1A2 variants) is the biggest factor. Smoking increases CYP1A2 activity, cutting half-life roughly in half. Pregnancy can extend half-life to 11-15 hours by the third trimester. Oral contraceptives roughly double caffeine half-life. Liver disease dramatically slows clearance. Medications like fluvoxamine, ciprofloxacin, and cimetidine inhibit CYP1A2. Age slightly slows metabolism in older adults. Diet affects it too — cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, cauliflower) induce CYP1A2, while grapefruit juice inhibits it. Even altitude has been shown to slow caffeine metabolism at elevations above 4,000 meters.
